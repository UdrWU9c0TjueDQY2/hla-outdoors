  1. Two weeks out Look up sunset for the date and the direction it sets from your spot

    Sunset moves a minute or two a day and the direction swings through the year. Pick the spot with the Sun setting inside the view you want behind you.

  2. The day before Walk the spot at the same hour and decide where each of you stands

    The photographer wants the Sun low and behind or beside the couple, never straight behind the camera. Mark the three spots on your sheet.

  3. 2 pm on the day Check cloud 60 to 180 km toward the setting Sun

    Low cloud on the horizon in the Sun's direction cuts the light before it turns gold. If the forecast there is grim, move the ask earlier or switch to the backup spot.

  4. Sunset minus 90 Arrive, let the nerves settle, confirm the photographer is in position

    Ninety minutes gives you the start of golden hour and room for a late bus. Leave the ring where you can reach it without a search.

  5. Sunset minus 60 Golden hour begins: the walk, the view, the warm-up photos

    The light is soft and the shadows are long. Do not ask yet; you will want this light for the photos afterwards.

  6. Sunset minus 30 The ask

    Roughly half an hour before the Sun touches the horizon is the sweet spot: full golden light for the moment, and twenty to thirty minutes of it left for photos with the ring on.

  7. Sunset to plus 25 Blue hour: quiet photos, the phone calls, the walk back

    The sky goes cobalt and the city lights come on. Twenty-five minutes, give or take, before it is simply dark.

Three kinds of light, one choice

Golden hour

Roughly the hour before sunset at mid-latitudes. Warm, soft, long shadows, faces lit. The photos people mean when they say "sunset proposal".

Sunset minus 30

Our pick for the ask. Golden light for the moment, and twenty to thirty minutes of it left for the photos that show the ring and both faces.

Blue hour

The twenty-odd minutes after the Sun goes. Cobalt sky, city lights, quiet. Lovely for the last frames, a hard place to see a ring.

Those figures are rules of thumb for the middle latitudes; golden hour stretches in summer and near the poles and shrinks toward the equator. The NOAA Solar Calculator gives the exact sunset for any spot and date, and a proposal photographer's guide like Valley Rose's golden hour piece lands on the same 30-to-60-minutes-before window. What those pages do not cover is the direction the Sun sets or the cloud on the horizon, which is where the run sheet and the app come in.

Thursday, 4:12 pm, a headland in October

A composite scenario, not a customer story.

Sam has the ring, a photographer friend called Nia, and a cliff path they walked on their second date. Sunset is 6:27. He picked the spot for the memory, not the light, and only this week thought to ask which way the Sun actually goes down from there. Asterfold's horizon view, turned toward the bench, shows the Sun setting 20° left of the frame, behind the headland, and the dates this year when it sets inside the frame are all in June. In October the bench is in shadow by 5:50.

So the ask moves forty metres up the path to the open rock, where the horizon view shows the Sun landing in the middle of a 60° frame tonight. Nia stands up-slope with the Sun to her right, so the couple glows and nobody squints. At 2 pm the forecast 60 km out toward the Sun is clear, 180 km is cloudy; that cloud will arrive after dark, so the plan holds. They arrive at 4:57, ninety minutes early, because the bus is the bus.

At 5:58, sunset minus 29, Sam stops at the rock. Nia has twenty-six minutes of gold afterwards and uses all of them. At 6:51 the sky is cobalt, the ring is on, and the first phone call is to Sam's mother, who already knew, because her name was on the sheet.

Who this is not for

  • Indoor or restaurant proposals. The light in there is yours to control already.
  • Anyone proposing at sunrise. Same arithmetic, reversed; the sheet's run order assumes an evening.
  • Spots hemmed in by hills or buildings. Asterfold assumes a flat horizon, and so does the sheet. Walk it the day before; that step is on the sheet for a reason.
  • People who want a guarantee. The app's ratings are labelled as estimates and we will not pretend otherwise. Thin cloud goes pink; low cloud on the horizon does not.

Questions people ask

How long before sunset should I propose?

About thirty minutes before the Sun touches the horizon. Golden hour has started, the light is warm and soft on faces, and you still have twenty to thirty minutes of it for photos with the ring on. Proposing at the moment of sunset looks right on paper and leaves you with the dark.

What is golden hour, exactly?

The stretch when the Sun is low and its light comes through more atmosphere, going warm and soft. At mid-latitudes it is roughly the hour before sunset; it runs longer in summer and at higher latitudes, shorter near the equator. Asterfold draws it on a sky dome for your place and date, and the NOAA Solar Calculator gives the raw sunset time if you would rather do the arithmetic.

Where should the photographer stand for a sunset proposal?

With the Sun low and behind or to the side of you both, so the sky is the backdrop and your faces catch the glow. If the Sun is straight behind the camera, you are squinting into it. If it is straight behind you, you are silhouettes, which is lovely for one frame and useless for the one where you can see the ring. Walk it the day before at the same hour.

What if it is cloudy?

Thin high cloud is a gift; it goes pink. Low cloud sitting on the horizon in the direction of the Sun is the problem, because it swallows the light before it turns gold. Check the forecast 60 to 180 km toward the setting Sun around 2 pm. Asterfold shows exactly those two point forecasts for tonight. If they are grim, move the ask earlier into the brighter light or use your backup spot.

Does Asterfold plan the proposal?

No. It tells you when and where the Sun sets from the exact spot you save, lets you turn toward the view and pick a lens so you can see whether the Sun lands in your frame, and rates tonight's sunset with plain-English reasons. The run sheet on this page is ours; the Sun data is the app's. The plan is yours.
