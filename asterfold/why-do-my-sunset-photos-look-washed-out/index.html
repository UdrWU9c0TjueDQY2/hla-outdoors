Three ways people chase a sunset

Point and hope

Arrive at sunset, shoot the Sun, leave as it goes. The photo is a white blob over a grey sky, and the best colour happens in the car park.

Edit it later

Pull the saturation up until the sky is a colour that never existed. It looks like it, too.

Plan the light

Know when golden hour starts and when blue hour ends, know which way the Sun goes down from your spot, check the cloud toward it, and expose for the sky. Most of that is timing, and timing is free.

Questions people ask

What is the best time to take sunset photos on a phone?

Two windows: the last 20 minutes before the Sun touches the horizon, for warm light on faces and landscapes, and the 10 to 25 minutes after it goes, for colour in the sky. The second one is the window people miss. The counter on this page gives both for your location.

Why are my sunset photos orange instead of pink?

Usually the phone's automatic white balance warming the whole frame, or you shot too early, when the light is orange, rather than after sunset, when it turns pink. Try a frame with exposure pulled down and one taken fifteen minutes later.

Should I turn HDR off for sunsets?

Try it. HDR keeps detail in both sky and ground, at the cost of contrast. For a sunset, a darker single exposure is often the stronger picture. Not every iPhone lets you switch it off; Apple's settings guide linked above lists what your model has.

How does the countdown know where I am?

Only if you tap the button and allow it. The browser hands the page a rough position, the page does the sun arithmetic itself, and nothing is sent anywhere; there is no server behind this page. You can type a latitude and longitude instead. The times assume a flat horizon.

Six reasons, most common first

1

You shot at sunset, not after it

The sky usually does its best work 10 to 25 minutes after the Sun has gone, when the light is coming up from under the horizon and the high cloud turns pink. Most people photograph the Sun itself, then leave as the colour arrives.

What helps: Stay. Use the timer below: the blue-hour countdown is the window most people walk out of.

2

The phone exposed for the Sun

A phone camera averages the scene. A bright Sun in frame pulls everything else toward grey and the sky loses its colour.

What helps: Tap the sky away from the Sun, then slide the little sun icon down to darken the shot by a stop or so. On iPhone, holding the tap locks exposure so it stops changing between frames. Apple's camera guide covers the controls.

3

HDR flattened it

Smart HDR merges a bright and a dark exposure so you can see both the sky and the ground. That is clever, and it is also why the sky looks like a postcard from 1994: the contrast that made it dramatic has been averaged away.

What helps: Shoot a second frame with the exposure pulled down, as above, and compare. On some iPhones you can turn Smart HDR off in Settings, Camera; the Apple page below says which.

4

You pointed at the Sun

The most saturated colour at sunset is often behind you: the pink band above the eastern horizon (the Belt of Venus) and the warm light on the land. Facing the Sun gives you a white blob and a silhouette.

What helps: Turn around for one frame. Then shoot the view you came for.

5

Low cloud on the horizon ate the light

Thin, high cloud makes a sunset. A bank of low cloud 60 to 180 km away in the direction of the Sun cuts the light before it gets warm, and no camera setting fixes that.

What helps: Check the forecast toward the setting Sun earlier in the day. Asterfold shows point forecasts 60 and 180 km toward the Sun for tonight, from MET Norway, with a plain-English rating.

6

Haze and the wrong season

Summer haze scatters the light into a pale wash; clear, cold, dry air after a front has passed gives the deep reds. Latitude matters too: near the equator the whole thing is over in minutes.

What helps: Watch for the day after rain. And lower your expectations in a July heatwave; it is not the phone.
