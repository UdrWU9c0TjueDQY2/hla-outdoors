Three kinds of light, one choice

Low sunlight

Look for the moment when the low Sun lights the couple from the side. Observe the view at the same time on a scouting visit.

A rehearsal time

Try thirty minutes before local sunset as one possible appointment. Check how much light actually reaches the spot.

After sunset

As twilight deepens, check exposure again before taking the last portraits. Set a finish time that suits the return route.

Sunset calculations are estimates. The date, latitude, local horizon and atmosphere all matter when planning the photograph. See NOAA’s calculation notes and use the sunset departure planner to work backward from your own chosen time.

Thursday, 4:12 pm, a headland in October

A composite scenario, not a customer story.

Sam has the ring, a photographer friend called Nia, and a cliff path they walked on their second date. Sunset is 6:27. He picked the spot for the memory, not the light, and only this week thought to ask which way the Sun actually goes down from there. Asterfold's horizon view, turned toward the bench, shows the Sun setting 20° left of the frame, behind the headland, and the dates this year when it sets inside the frame are all in June. In October the bench is in shadow by 5:50.

So the ask moves forty metres up the path to the open rock, where the horizon view shows the Sun landing in the middle of a 60° frame tonight. Nia stands up-slope with the Sun to her right, so the couple glows and nobody squints. At 2 pm the forecast 60 km out toward the Sun is clear, 180 km is cloudy; the forecast alone does not tell them when the cloud will reach the viewpoint. They keep the backup available. They arrive at 4:57, ninety minutes early, because the bus is the bus.

At 5:58, sunset minus 29, Sam stops at the rock. Nia has twenty-six minutes of gold afterwards and uses all of them. At 6:51 the sky is cobalt, the ring is on, and the first phone call is to Sam's mother, who already knew, because her name was on the sheet.

Who this is not for

  • Indoor or restaurant proposals. The light in there is yours to control already.
  • Anyone proposing at sunrise. Same arithmetic, reversed; the sheet's run order assumes an evening.
  • Spots hemmed in by hills or buildings. Asterfold assumes a flat horizon, and so does the sheet. Walk it the day before; that step is on the sheet for a reason.
  • People who want a guarantee. The app's ratings are labelled as estimates and we will not pretend otherwise. Cloud cover and the local horizon can change the actual view.

Questions people ask

How long before sunset should I propose?

Choose the moment after scouting the viewpoint at a similar time. Thirty minutes before sunset is one example for a rehearsal; allow time for photos and a comfortable return.

What is golden hour, exactly?

Golden hour describes low-angle sunlight. Its timing depends on the date and latitude, and a ridge or building can block the Sun earlier. Enter your own arrival and finish times on the sheet.

Where should the photographer stand for a sunset proposal?

With the Sun low and behind or to the side of you both, so the sky is the backdrop and your faces catch the glow. If the Sun is straight behind the camera, you are squinting into it. If it is straight behind you, you are silhouettes, which is lovely for one frame and useless for the one where you can see the ring. Walk it the day before at the same hour.

What if it is cloudy?

Check the local forecast before leaving, then look toward the actual horizon. If cloud or shade changes the light, move the moment earlier or choose a backup spot.

Does Asterfold plan the proposal?

No. It tells you when and where the Sun sets from the exact spot you save, lets you turn toward the view and pick a lens so you can see whether the Sun lands in your frame, and rates tonight's sunset with plain-English reasons. The run sheet on this page is ours; the Sun data is the app's. The plan is yours.
