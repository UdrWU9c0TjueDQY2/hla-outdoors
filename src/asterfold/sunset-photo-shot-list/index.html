    FOR CAMERA CLUBS & PHOTO WALKS

    One place. Three ways to see it.

    Try this as a small-group exercise: each person makes one wide view, one frame around a clear subject and one close detail. Then compare which choice changes the story most. A phone camera is enough.

    1. Choose a permitted, accessible viewpoint and agree on the return plan.
    2. Build a setup above and copy its link so everyone starts with the same prompts.
    3. Print a field sheet or keep the copied list in your notes.
    4. Afterward, let each person explain one framing decision. No scoring or competition is required.

    Clubs and educators may copy or print this original exercise for their activities. An app purchase, backlink or endorsement is not required. The separate Asterfold app is an optional next step.

    Move the camera, move the overlap

    Try a ten-minute exercise for two people. Make two photos and one crop to see which changes the overlap between a nearby edge and a distant landmark. A phone, a pencil and a mark on a far wall are enough.

    Read the complete exercise on this page

    Picture guide. The two drawings show a twig against a distant dot. The twig overlaps the dot in frame A. In frame B, the far dot is in the same part of the picture and the twig appears to its left.

    1. Line up two features. Choose a near vertical edge and a distant fixed mark. Frame them so the edge covers the mark. Pick one zoom setting and take photo A.
    2. Predict, step, compare. Predict which side of the edge will reveal the mark. Move the phone a little sideways on level ground, keep the same zoom and centre the far mark again. Take photo B. A seated pair can slide the phone between two supported positions on a table.
    3. Change the crop, keep the spot. Crop photo B around the mark. Compare the overlap in the crop with photo B, then compare A with B. Which change moved the edge?

    Discuss. What did you predict and what happened? Which frame would you keep? What distracts from the subject in the other one?

    MAKE THE FRAMES DIFFERENT

    Start wide.

    For a panorama, plan the number of photos and the turn between frames from your camera’s horizontal coverage.

    Show enough of the setting to explain where you are. Try giving either the sky or foreground more room, then compare the result.

    Choose an anchor.

    Find one shape you want someone to notice first. Keep an eye on where it touches the edges of the frame or other shapes.

    Finish small.

    A reflected colour, lit window or patch of bark can make a different picture from another wide skyline. Use what the evening actually gives you.

    Does this predict sunset time or colour?

    No. It only creates composition prompts. It does not use location, weather or astronomy data, and it does not guarantee a visible sunset.

    Do I need a dedicated camera?

    No. The prompts are about framing, so you can try them with a phone or a camera. This tool does not suggest device-specific exposure settings.

    Will my checklist be saved?

    Checked-off shots are not saved. Copy the list to your notes or print it before leaving. A setup link restores your three choices, but not checklist progress. Resetting returns to the example setup.

    What should I check before going?

    Confirm that access is permitted, check local conditions and opening hours, and plan your return route. Keep your position safe; a composition prompt is never a reason to approach an edge, water, traffic or a restricted area.

    Once the shot list is ready, work backward from your checked sunset time and save the schedule to a calendar. Build a departure timeline.
