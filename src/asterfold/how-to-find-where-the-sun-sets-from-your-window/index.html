Questions from the window

Which direction does the sun set?

West, roughly, and the roughly matters. At the equinoxes in March and September it sets almost due west everywhere. Between them it swings: at London’s latitude the sunset point moves from about 230° (south-west) on 21 December to about 310° (north-west) on 21 June, an 80-degree swing. Nearer the equator the swing is smaller; further north or south it is larger.

Does the sun set in the same place every day?

No. It moves a little each day, fastest around the equinoxes and slowest around the solstices, when it pauses and turns back. That is why a window that catches the sunset in November can miss it entirely in June.

How do I know if the sun will set in view of my window?

Three methods, in order of effort. Watch and mark: stand at the window at sunset for a few evenings and note where it drops; exact for your window, including the buildings, but only for those dates. Compass rule: read the window’s facing with a compass app, then compare with the sunset bearing for the date (the quiz above gives one). Sunset planner: Asterfold saves the spot, shows a first-person horizon and lists the dates this year when the Sun sets inside your frame.

What about the buildings and hills in the way?

The compass rule and Asterfold both assume a flat horizon; the listing says so plainly, hills, buildings and trees are not modelled. If a tower sits where the Sun should drop, only method one, watching, tells you that. Use the bearing to know the month, then look.

Is the bearing from the quiz exact?

It is the standard flat-horizon formula with a rounded latitude band, so expect a few degrees either way; refraction and your real horizon move the true point a little. It is plenty to tell a south-west window from a north-west one. Asterfold runs the full calculation for your exact spot and date.
